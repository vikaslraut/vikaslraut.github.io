01 · Why now

The one FOMO-led decision I feel good about

The grind

Smarsh/Cyber has been exhausting for months — 60-hour work weeks were the norm. The way of working there was its own controversy, not the scope of this deck, but every one of my earlier attempts at AI-assisted development failed. I know why now. If I could time-travel six months back with the lessons I hold today, I'd do it differently.

The spark

Claude Sonnet 4.6's training cutoff was August 2025. After that, the dev forums exploded over how far it had pulled ahead in AI-assisted coding. All reading and no hands-on was making Vikas a dull boy. The FOMO made me restless — so in March 2026, much later than I'd like to admit, I picked this project as my first independent experiment to truly live like a vibe-coder.

Most of my FOMO-led decisions are over-estimated. This one felt personally rewarding, and I feel obligated to share it — with the caveat that I've only scratched the surface. There's a lot more here to try and achieve.
02 · The context

SocDocLoc — a brief to set the context of this conversation

What it is

A simple document locker for housing societies, built on statutory mandates in the Model Bye-laws 2014 and the MCS Act 1960 — an act amended repeatedly across 66 years. Societies are required to audit themselves; the DDR is the authority meant to supervise, review, and control.

The pain

Most societies don't clear the audit; very few earn a Class A certificate. The scramble starts when matters turn legal, or when a society considers redevelopment — the cost of not maintaining statutory documents surfaces exactly when management committees expect it least. At the root of it: plain unawareness.

What it does

SocDocLoc digitizes every document a housing society is expected to maintain, leaving only 3 that still need to exist as physical copies. The property card initiative — a digital certificate of ownership — could eliminate the need for those 3 as well. We'll see how that pans out.

The win

Cleared our internal SOC2 Type II audit and DPDP Act compliance. Both onboarded societies cleared their audit with a DDR-attested Class A certificate. We now have the proof — it's up to the sales team to take it ahead from here. My job (sort of) ends here.

03 · The thesis

My thesis — I will not write a single line of code, but will review all of it

The thesis

Picking up signals from the dev forums, I formed my own thesis on how a greenfield app could take birth — from idea, to definition, to execution, to deployment, to onboarding real customers. Not a single line of code written by hand, but every line of AI-generated code reviewed.

Easy enough for a greenfield app. Probably not so easy on a 5+ year legacy codebase with nothing documented. The thesis had to be tested before I started coding.

Where it came from

It stemmed from the "good" projects I'd worked on at Caizin and in my past life — the 4E rehaul above all, 14 months with 4–5 engineers. Specs lived in Jira and Confluence, mostly written by MJ, then synthesized into developer-focused epics and stories, each linked back to the original definition.

Getting started

That experience of what a "good" SDLC looks like added real value to the thesis. I started small — just a password-less login screen. Frontend on React/shadcn, backend on C# services, Postgres for the database, AWS SES for emails. The project folder structure took shape from there.

04 · Getting real

How do I "really" start? — the part no one warns you about with spec-driven, AI-assisted build-out

Where I got stuck

I acted like a typical engineer — got my tiers and layers ready in VS Code. But starting an actual feature build-out is where I first felt lost.

If this was spec-driven + AI, it became clear the spec had to live closer to the code. This time, in the GitHub repo — not Jira. Not yet.

The key was to start with definition

  • What is the product expected to do?
  • What business model does it serve?
  • How do we expect users to interact with it?
  • Why would users follow a certain workflow?
  • What are my domain entities, and how crisply can I define their relationships?
  • How are features laid out — who (role) does what, where, and how?
A high-level definition was hand-written. Tell the AI everything it needs to know — serve the context it needs on a platter. The WHY comes in the slides that follow.
05 · The writing

3 weeks of just "writing"

How I wrote

  • Not Shashi Tharoor style — plain, basic English.
  • Highly accurate, complete sentences.
  • Instruction-style, without unnecessarily personifying Claude.

PS: Claude never takes offense, is never rude, is never sad — but it also never pushes back, it keeps obeying... until it doesn't. So don't abuse it when it fails; reinforce it when it does well. That's how output quality compounds.

What I wrote

  • Defined the problem — sometimes over and over again.
  • Defined every word in the domain nomenclature, and let the model trace it back to the Model Bye-laws and MCS Act (both public PDFs).
  • Collected all artifacts and supported them with evidence.
  • Described the app's behavior, literally.
  • Described every detail of user interaction.
06 · The WHY

Describing the solution architecture — like a "good" (engineer) boy

Beyond WHAT and HOW

I don't recall the last time I wrote this much about my engineering decisions. This time it went beyond the WHAT and the HOW, into the WHY — in plain terms, ADRs: a decision, its reasoning, and what I didn't choose. The payoff wasn't just for me: the same WHY documentation became context Claude drew on across later sessions, keeping decisions consistent long after the reasoning had left short-term memory.

Decision → rationale

Encryption at restNot a default habit — required for PII, not optional.
Secure transfer, alwaysNo exceptions carved out for "internal network."
PII always maskedSame obligation, enforced at the query layer.
Secrets: where + rotationWritten down so rotation is a schedule, not a memory.
Automated vs. backend jobInvisible-to-the-user work still needs a named owner.

What we ruled out

Not every decision stuck. One call — argued from preference rather than evidence — got revisited and flipped, twice, after implementation exposed what the reasoning had missed. Writing the WHY down is what made the reversal cheap: the record showed exactly what to undo, and why the original logic didn't hold.

A side thought: CMMI Level 5 and Six Sigma companies should be having a field day with AI right now — they hand-wrote every decision, with real traceability behind most of their ADRs. I wonder why their stories are still invisible.
07 · The alignment check

The meeting-room simulation — the great alignment

Still no code

Still no real code written. I entered a "meeting room" with Claude and asked it to play back everything it understood of the product so far — how deeply it understood what was to be built, and how well it could synthesize my spec instructions.

What I expected vs. what I got

I asked it to question me, to poke holes and surface the gaps in my spec. It did not. I had to be explicit that I could see the gaps myself — I needed it to dig deeper, and to improve and organize the spec documents.

A guided tour of the shelf

The schema, handled like evidence. 55 canonical migration files — one per table — run in order, journaled by filename: an applied file is never edited again. Every DDL change gets a second look from a dedicated schema review before it runs, lands in the schema changelog, and periodic snapshots record what the schema actually was, not what the docs remember.

The specs — the newest resident. A spec.md per feature (every gap tagged [ASSUMED] until a human answers) plus a plan.md ticket roadmap, built from a shared template, diffed and reviewed like code. The rule that keeps them honest: a behavior change ships with its spec change in the same PR.

The context graph. INDEX → STORY_MAP → 15 domain nodes, each ~30–50 lines, kept current after every unit of work. Its own rule, verbatim: "a lying map is worse than no map."

The monoliths, indexed not split. 912 KB of technical docs stay append-only; a 27 KB index routes into them — 34:1. Nobody reads them end-to-end; nobody is supposed to.

The sharp edge. A mode flag plus a pre-edit hook: while mode reads "implementing," edits to test files are denied. Git hooks and review agents round out the enforcement.

10 · The pipeline

Clarify an epic once. Then every story is execution, not archaeology.

Level 1 · Epic — runs once
01/start-story — full context + coverage check, never code.
02/specify — behavioral spec, every gap tagged [ASSUMED].
03/clarify — one question at a time, until none remain.
04plan.md — the ticket roadmap, approved once, then it drives.
Level 2 · Story — per ticket

How we came to this — three versions, all paid for

v1 — the 6-step loop (build one, April): spec → build → verify → document, one unit per turn. Good instincts, no teeth: nothing could refuse anything.

v2 — the 9-step method: added clarify-before-build and docs-in-the-same-changeset. Still policy — the test strategy it relied on was believed for 107 days while zero test files existed.

The reckoning: a marketing draft claimed a "test-first habit" — a repo grep said no. Two days later a routine session drifted into live code edits and was reverted with an incident report. The process plan written that week opens with the gap list: "No tests. Zero."

v3 — this pipeline (versioned like code): the epic level exists because ticket-by-ticket clarification kept re-asking the spec; the tests-first gate exists because of the 92%; the review gate exists because the real catches all came from fresh context; the coverage check exists because the tracker arrived mid-project and ticket status doesn't equal feature existence.

There is no final version. The process file carries a dated changelog at the bottom for exactly that reason.

The quarter, told straight

March. Before a single line of product code: 67 files, 14 MB — the governing act, the bye-laws, photographs of the paper registers. Changelog v1: "Initial baseline. Research complete." The one thing done on time.

April. First commit on the 3rd. Live deploy on the 22nd — day 19, nine commits, none a feature. The speed was real. So was what it quietly skipped: the test strategy written on the 27th never became a gate.

May–June. Production stack by day 32. Growth — and sediment: a 352 KB "state" file, an 89-day-stale search hook, a commit convention nobody enforced, docs that started disagreeing with themselves.

July 16–18. The reckoning. "Is this we did?" — a grep says the test-first habit is fiction. A session drifts into edits it was never allowed to make. In three days: the contract file cut 68%, the state file archived, the nudge hook deleted, the deny hook installed, the commit check finally wired, the two-level pipeline lands.

Since. 58 test files in 9 days, green across the board, every unit through the gates. Not because discipline was asked for — because it finally had machinery.

And the method flowed backwards: an earlier build imported this system's standards three weeks after it started — its entire process created in a single commit. Another build became the tooling experiment in between.

The dated receipts

Human catches, in the developer's own words: "was there no API change expected?" · "no we need high confidence" — found a table missing a sequence column entirely · "wait … i dont think resolution are getting deleted" — a fix just certified, while the container was still serving stale code · a burst-email reversal · "wait i feel we are drifting" · "is docs/context/domains updated?" — and more, each logged.

Fresh-context review catches (4): a MIME normalisation mismatch after the fix shipped · an early return silently disabling two brand-new statutory rules · an upload limit hardcoded in one place, configurable in two others · one result type quietly swapped for another — catching the implementer's own certified fix. Review also polices the design system: a drift audit found three different delete-button styles on one page; another review flagged 28 instances of token arithmetic across 7 files.

What the test gate cost, on the record: a locked test file itself had the bug (a bigint count coerced to int) · one rule-flip needed two locked suites unlocked, one unknown until mid-unit · a shared, no-teardown test database caused three cross-class collisions — the fixture is still unfixed, a real trade, carried knowingly.

The names: separation of duties · four-eyes · TDD red–green (Beck, 2002) · detective vs. preventive controls. The variable that mattered was never intelligence — it was having no stake and no context debt.

13 · Evidence: what drifts

So write it down, surely? We wrote everything down.

Everything unenforced drifted. Everything.

the docs

Line 12: "never store raw [national ID]." Line 57: an ALTER that stores it raw — deliberate, logged. 45 lines apart. Same file. Both still there.

the conventions

The commit convention shipped on day 2. Enforcement landed on day 107. In between: a 466-character subject line, 17% adherence.

the automation

A hook told every search to "consult the graph first." The graph was stale 83 of its 89 days. The fix wasn't a repair — it was deletion.

the watcher (build three)

The full drift ledger

Docs: four sources in the repo still disagree about which storage is live — including the contract file contradicting itself ten lines apart. What finally worked wasn't location, it was definition of done: the post-unit step updates the docs in the same changeset.

Files: every file given a numeric cap stayed under it (one context file: 352,562 bytes → archived → a 60-line hard cap). The two files without a number are now 853 KB and 480 KB. A target set as a number gets beaten; a target set as a sentence gets believed.

Build one, the control group: no message hook — five commits whose text is the literal shell command that was meant to produce them, unterminated quote and all.

The watcher, in full: build three logged 62% of its telemetry after its last commit, recorded the wrong stack at confidence 70, and never used its own corrections mechanism — its installer silently repointed the shared hooks path, disabling the neighbouring repo's guardrails, then reported the resulting "low commit quality" as the developer's problem. Its own design constraint, verbatim: "Confident wrong answers are worse than no answers."

The honest holes, and the bill

Even the database has two holes: object-storage keys written before the tenant prefix carry no tenant information — ever again, for those objects. And three columns appended to an already-journaled migration existed on no database at all — found as a live 500: column does not exist. Forward-only migrations were re-learned at retail price.

The corpus paid off in WHERE clauses: the duplicate-number clash guard filtered on an active-member flag — ceased members invisible, a statutory number reissuable on an official document. The fix came from knowing the register, not the code. And the spec learned: a rule specified as one-time became re-runnable when real usage invalidated the premise.

The human's operating line, verbatim: "I have a READ-ONLY IAM key… give me one command at a time, I run it with my admin creds, you verify before the next." Its bill, logged unprompted: 7 logical commits' worth of uncommitted work in one session. Every guardrail has a bill. We print ours.

15 · The model
cost of a mistake = blast radius × time to detection

Every witness you just heard shrinks one of the two terms. Hooks and REVOKEs shrink radius. Reviews and humans shrink detection time. And the 14 MB corpus does the only thing nothing else can: it catches the mistake before it exists — detection time goes negative.

Boehm published this curve in 1981. We re-derived it in 2026 — at retail price.

We did not discover this It is a restatement of Boehm's cost-of-change curve — Software Engineering Economics, 1981, p. 40. The curve is 45 years old. What's new is not the curve. What's new is how cheap it became to generate the mistakes that ride it.
16 · The verdict

The diff can't tell. The caller can.

Two identical diffs — and the difference lived in the transaction wrapped around them. It scales all the way up. The quality of this system never lived in its code. It lived in:

  • The hook that refuses — so the writer can't grade their own work.
  • The database grant that makes the illegal state unrepresentable.
  • The reviewer with no stake and no context debt.
  • The human who knows which day all the emails go out.
  • The corpus that knew what correct meant before the first commit.
Production of code stopped being the constraint. Judgment, definition, and structure did not — and nothing distributed those the way it distributed code.

18 · Where do I stand on my thesis?

Where do I stand on my thesis?

What held up
  • Detailed spec + context + artifacts.
  • AI-assisted spec organization, with human review.
  • Smaller prompts.
  • Smaller, controlled changesets — more commits, good GitHub comments.
  • Manageable code reviews.

Impossible to review. I swear, I tried.

UI design

Hard to describe in words — try a Figma input next time.

The thesis mostly holds — write enough spec, review everything, and it works. What it didn't predict: "reviewable" isn't evenly distributed across the stack. That unevenness is where judgment still earns its keep.
